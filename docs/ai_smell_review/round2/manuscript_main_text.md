# Predicting how repetition penalties act in language models from unpenalized samples

## Abstract

[P1] A repetition penalty in a language model is applied, and the distribution renormalized, at every sampling step. We predict its effect on how generated text switches between repetitive and ordinary stretches, using only samples generated without the penalty, and show that this first-order prediction holds at penalties strong enough to change the switching. We measure switching by the log ratio of the rates of entering and leaving labeled repetitive stretches. The penalty we study adds h to the logits of repeat candidates, the tokens that have previously followed the current last two tokens. At h = 0, the derivative of a text's log-probability with respect to h is (N − Q)/T. Here N counts the candidates chosen, Q sums over steps the probability of choosing one, and T is the temperature. Each term of Q is fixed by the text before its step, yet Q covaries with the switching statistics almost as strongly as N does. Step-wise normalization therefore cancels about 99% of the first-order response the same penalty would produce on whole texts. It removes the credit each choice would receive for the repeats it makes likely later. After a first test that did not decide, we replicated on new news articles in seven open base models, with the analysis registered in advance. With the derivative estimated from the unpenalized runs of the same test, the realized change divided by the prediction had a median of 1.04, with its bootstrap interval over articles inside the registered range of 0.8 to 1.25. A separate test on two larger models also fell inside the range. The widely used multiplicative penalty followed the prediction only up to the weakest strengths used in practice. Although the change in the log ratio was predicted, the temperature at which the entry and exit rates are equal, which the design targeted, did not always move as intended. The derivative also shows that the response arises mainly at the minority of steps where the model is uncertain whether to repeat.

## I. Introduction

[P2] Language models that sample one token at a time sometimes fall into loops, repeating a phrase or a sentence until the end of the output. This problem is old and persists in current systems [1904.09751; 2012.14660; 2206.02369; 2310.10226; 2512.12895]. Repetition reinforces itself: the more often a phrase has been repeated, the more likely it is to be repeated again [2206.02369]. Adaptive sampling schemes have been designed partly to avoid it [2007.14966]. Training objectives that discourage repetition have been proposed [1908.04319]. At inference, the usual remedy is to penalize tokens that would repeat earlier text [1909.05858], although in a production setting such a penalty removed only some kinds of repetition [2512.04419]. Penalties derived from a model of expected frequencies have also been proposed [2607.22694].

[P3] Sampling at temperature T draws each token with probability proportional to exp(z/T), where z are the logits. Several studies have therefore described generated text in the language of statistical physics, with phases and transitions as the temperature changes [2406.05335; 2503.06330; 2405.17088; 2606.06238]. In small models, repetitive structure has been reported to appear only in a low-temperature ordered phase [2406.05335; 2503.06330]. These works describe generated text but do not predict how an intervention such as a penalty acts. A natural shortcut would treat the penalty as an energy per repeat in a two-state Boltzmann system. Appendix H shows why this shortcut is not a reliable guide.

[P4] The Boltzmann form holds for each step and not for the text as a whole. At T = 1 the probability of a text is exp(−U(x)), with U the sum over its tokens of −log p at T = 1. At any other temperature, or with any change to the logits, the distribution is normalized afresh at every step [2503.21929]. The probability of a text is then, in general, not exp(−U(x)/T)/Z(T) for an additive energy U that is independent of T. The response of such a process to a change in its transition probabilities is given exactly by a likelihood-ratio, or score-function, formula [Glynn 1990; Williams 1992; Warren & Allen 2012]. We use this formula to predict the effect of a penalty from unpenalized samples, and test the predictions after registering them. With the same formula we measure how much of a whole-text, Boltzmann-like response survives step-wise normalization, which explains why the effect is small, and locate where in a text a penalty acts.

[P5] The formula is standard in simulation and reinforcement learning. The difference between step-wise and whole-text normalization has been studied in several fields. In sequence labeling, locally normalized models can be biased in ways that globally normalized ones are not, a problem known as label bias [Lafferty et al. 2001; Andor et al. 2016; Wiseman & Rush 2016]. In statistical physics, reweighting whole trajectories gives tilted ensembles, which can be written step by step as Doob transforms [Garrahan et al. 2009; Jack & Sollich 2010; Chetrite & Touchette 2015]. Language-model decoding has been steered by estimates of a future reward or attribute [Yang & Klein 2021; Zhao et al. 2024], and Kempton and Burrell described how local normalization distorts temperature and truncation relative to a global Boltzmann form [2503.21929]. Here we bring these ideas to a repetition penalty and test the resulting prediction prospectively.

## II. Setup

[P6] We sampled continuations of news articles published after the models were released, from nine open base models between 0.4 and 7.6 billion parameters (Table I; Appendix A). Sampling was plain softmax at temperatures between 0.19 and 0.90. Each test used seven temperatures per model, centered on a design temperature chosen for that model (Sec. IV B), with no truncation such as top-k or top-p. End-of-sequence and padding tokens were excluded, and all probabilities below, including the candidate probability q_t defined next, refer to this masked distribution. Each prompt was the first 64 tokens of an article, continued for 512 tokens. We call one round of computing the next-token logits, adjusting them, normalizing them and sampling one token a step.

[P7] **Repeats and penalties.** A sampled token is a repeat if the four tokens ending with it occurred earlier in the same continuation, that is, if it ends a repeated 4-gram. The penalty acts on a slightly broader set. At step t the repeat candidates are the tokens that have previously followed the current last two tokens, in the prompt or the continuation. We write c_t = 1 if the sampled token is a candidate and q_t for the total probability that the model, at temperature T and without the penalty, assigns to the candidates. Repeats define the states below, and candidates define the penalty. Keeping them separate makes the definition of the states independent of the penalty. The additive penalty adds h to the logits of the candidates before division by T, so that h < 0 discourages repetition. This additive penalty is not an option of common libraries. It acts like a logit bias on a set that depends on the context, and we use it because its exact score has the simplest form. A widely used multiplicative penalty, implemented as repetition_penalty in common libraries, acts on a third set, every token seen before: before division by T, it divides their positive logits by θ and multiplies their negative logits by θ [1909.05858; 1910.03771].

[P8] **Two states and the crossover temperature.** Repeats cluster into long stretches separated by ordinary text. We label each step as repetitive or ordinary from the fraction of repeats in a 32-token window around the step, 16 tokens before it and 15 after. The label has hysteresis: a step enters the repetitive state when the fraction reaches 1/2 and leaves it when the fraction falls below 1/4 (Appendix A). Each label therefore uses up to 15 later tokens. At each temperature we count switches into the repetitive state per ordinary step, the entry rate, and switches out of it per repetitive step, the exit rate. Their ratio, K = entry rate/exit rate, measures the balance between the two states. We call the temperature at which K = 1 the crossover temperature T_×. Near T_×, ln K is close to linear in 1/T, as in a van 't Hoff plot, and over wider ranges it curves. An analysis uses a temperature only if both kinds of switch occurred there at least ten times. We call these the usable temperatures.

[P9] The two states are a labeling, not distinct phases. The windowed repeat fraction has peaks near zero and one, but it did not meet a bimodality criterion we had fixed in advance (Appendix F1). K is also not the rate ratio of a stationary two-state Markov chain. The balance drifts towards repetition along a text, and repetitive stretches become harder to leave the longer they last (Appendix F2). We use K as a summary of switching statistics, averaged over positions and over stretches of different duration. T_× depends on the labeling and on the length of the continuation, so we compare it only within a fixed labeling and length.

## III. Theory

[P10] **A. Score of a penalized text.** A continuation x = (x_1, …, x_L) has probability P_h(x) = Π_t p_t^h(x_t | x_<t). For the additive penalty, the derivative of log p_t^h(x_t) at h = 0 is (c_t − q_t)/T. Summing over steps,

[P11] ∂ log P_h(x)/∂h |_{h=0} = (N − Q)/T,  with N = Σ_t c_t and Q = Σ_t q_t.

[P12] Here the length L is fixed, with no early stopping; this identity also holds with a bounded stopping time, such as an end-of-sequence token kept in the normalization, but we did not use one. N is the number of candidates chosen. Q is its compensator, the sum of the probabilities of choosing a candidate given the text so far: each q_t is fixed by the text before step t, and N − Q has mean zero. For any statistic O of the text that does not itself depend on h, the response at h = 0 is therefore

[P13] ∂E[O]/∂h = Cov(O, N)/T − Cov(O, Q)/T.

[P14] Applied to the four counts that define K, this gives the exact derivative g(T) = ∂ ln K/∂h, where K is defined by ratios of expected counts (Appendix B). For the multiplicative penalty, with λ = ln θ, c_t − q_t is replaced by a_t − E_t[a] (Appendix B). Here a is the derivative of a token's logit with respect to λ, and E_t the expectation under the step-t distribution.

[P15] **B. Step-wise penalty versus exponential tilting.** A penalty that acted on the text as a whole would instead reweight it. Reweighting each whole text by exp(hN/T) and renormalizing is exponential tilting in the language of statistics, and a tilted ensemble of trajectories in statistical physics. It gives the response Cov(O, N)/T, the static susceptibility of the path ensemble. The step-wise response equals this tilted response minus Cov(O, Q)/T. To see what the difference means, write N − E[N] as the sum of updates of the expected final count, E[N | x_≤t] − E[N | x_<t]. Each update is c_t − q_t plus ν_t. Here ν_t is the change, on learning x_t, in the expected number of candidates chosen after step t, and it has mean zero given the earlier text. Summing over t gives N − E[N] = (N − Q) + Σ_t ν_t, so Q − E[Q] = Σ_t ν_t and

[P16] Cov(O, Q) = Cov(O, Σ_t ν_t).

[P17] Exponential tilting rewards each choice also for the future repeats it makes likely. Written step by step, the tilted ensemble is again an autoregressive process. Its step probabilities are multiplied by exp(hc_t/T) and by the expected future factor E[exp(hN_{>t}/T) | x_≤t], with N_{>t} the candidates chosen after step t, and renormalized. This is a finite-horizon Doob transform [Jack & Sollich 2010; Chetrite & Touchette 2015]. To first order in h, after renormalization, the expected future factor adds (h/T) Σ_t ν_t to the log-weight of a text, a term that a step-wise penalty lacks. Entering a repetitive stretch makes many future repeats likely, so this is where we expect the two to differ most. Writing g_N for the response of ln K under exponential tilting by exp(hN/T), we call s_pen = g/g_N the fraction of the tilted response that survives step-wise normalization.

[P18] **C. Temperature.** Changing the temperature is also a step-wise change. With β = 1/T and energy U_t = −log p_t at T = 1,

[P19] ∂ log P_β(x)/∂β = −(U − U_comp),  with U = Σ_t U_t(x_t) and U_comp = Σ_t E_t[U_t],

[P20] where E_t is the expectation under the step-t distribution at the current temperature. The step-wise response to temperature, which is what the van 't Hoff slope measures, therefore also subtracts a compensator. Exponential tilting by exp(−εU) does not. Because each step's expectation is subtracted, adding to U_t any constant that depends only on the history, for example replacing U_t by −z_t, leaves this score unchanged. The corresponding fraction for temperature is s_temp = b/b_tilt, where b_tilt is the response of ln K to exponential tilting by exp(−εU) at ε = 0. Over several temperatures we sum numerator and denominator with the weights of the equilibrium predictions of Sec. III D, s_temp = Σ_u g_C(T_u)/Σ_u g_C^tilt(T_u).

[P21] **D. An equilibrium shortcut.** A two-state Boltzmann model, with the penalty as an energy per chosen candidate, predicts the response from unpenalized samples as g_C(T) = (b/ΔE) Δc_T/T (Appendix H). Here ΔE is the difference in mean energy per step between the states, and Δc_T the difference in the rate of choosing a candidate. The prediction needs an energy for each step and changes with that choice; the exact identities and s_pen do not.

## IV. Results

### A. Two states and the crossover temperature

[P22] Consider a temperature where both kinds of stretch are common. There, the fraction of repeats in a 32-token window has a high peak near zero, a narrow peak at exactly one and a shallow trough in between (Fig. 1b). Most outputs switch between the two kinds at least once. In the repetitive state the models chose a repeat candidate at almost every step, and in the ordinary state at about one step in five (Appendix F1).

[P23] The crossover temperature lies between 0.44 and 0.66 for the nine models (Fig. 1c; Table I).

### B. Predicting a penalty before applying it

[P25] Penalizing or favoring the repeat candidates moved T_× in the direction of h, roughly linearly (Fig. S1). Because the derivative of Sec. III A needs only unpenalized samples, unpenalized runs on 40 design articles suffice to predict the penalty h* that should lower T_× by 0.10. We call T* = T_× − 0.10 the design temperature. Before generating any penalized text for these tests, we fixed the procedure, the analysis code and the decision criterion in dated documents. These documents did not cover Fig. S1, whose runs were made earlier. Each test compares the realized change in ln K at h* with the first-order prediction h* g(T), summed over the usable temperatures. Of the two factors of the prediction, only h* was fixed beforehand, while g came from the unpenalized runs of the same test. We required the bootstrap interval of the median ratio over models to lie between 0.8 and 1.25.

[P26] The first test, on the 40 articles from which h* had been computed, did not decide: its intervals at h* and at 2h* both reached just above 1.25 (Appendix F3). We then committed in advance to a single replication on 120 new articles, with the same rule and statistic. Its median ratio was 1.04 (95% interval 0.98–1.15), inside the range (Fig. 2; Table I). For comparison, the tilted response of Sec. IV C would give a ratio of about 0.01. The equilibrium two-state model, evaluated after the tests, would have fallen outside the range for six of the nine models (Appendix H). Five of the nine per-model intervals reached above 1.25 (Table I). Under eight other labelings of the states the median stayed inside the range (Table F2), and across temperatures the ratio did not drift systematically (Appendix F10). A separate test repeated the whole procedure for two 7-billion-parameter models, where the rule applied to the mean ratio, 0.96 (0.84–1.11).

[P27] The same procedure predicted the divisor θ of the multiplicative penalty. At the predicted divisors the median ratio over seven models was 1.01, inside the range. With ln θ set to twice its predicted value the ratio was still 1.00, but at eight times it fell below the range (Appendix F5). The prediction therefore holds only for divisors up to about 1.1, the weakest values used in practice. For two instruction-tuned models the test did not decide or could not be run.

[P28] A user would care about where T_× ends up. With T_× from a straight-line fit of ln K against 1/T, as in the design, it moved further than intended, by a median of 1.3 times the intended shift in the smaller models. Because ln K curves (Fig. 1c), we also located each crossing by interpolation after the tests. This brought the median to 0.87, so curvature accounts for most of the overshoot (Appendix F6). Individual models, GPT-2-XL most of all, and the larger models still departed from the target, for reasons we have not identified.

### C. Step-wise normalization cancels almost all of the tilted response

[P31] Compared with what the same penalty would do if it reweighted whole texts, the responses predicted and confirmed above are small. Table II compares each step-wise response with the response under exponential tilting, on the unpenalized runs of the replication and of the test on the larger models. For the penalty, the step-wise response of ln K was between 0.7% and 1.5% of the tilted response (s_pen), and for temperature it was at most 2.3% (s_temp). Thus the compensator term Cov(O, Q)/T cancels about 99% of the tilted term Cov(O, N)/T. Both fractions are first-order quantities, taken at zero penalty and zero tilt. Nearly all of the covariance of the switching counts with N comes through the future repeats that each choice makes likely, and Q carries the same expected future repeats (Sec. III B).

[P32] These fractions use covariances within articles. Pooling the covariances of the step-wise response over articles, or computing s_temp from the step-wise temperature identity instead of the slope b, changes them little. Shorter texts keep a larger fraction, 1.8% for the first 256 tokens, consistent with fewer future repeats following each choice (Appendices B and F8). Both fractions are exploratory (Table C1).

Unlike s_pen, s_temp depends on the energy assigned to a step. The factors relating these fractions to the equilibrium shortcut are in Appendix H.

### D. Where the penalty acts

[P34] Each step contributes c_t − q_t to the score, a term with variance q_t(1 − q_t). Steps at which the model has almost decided to repeat, with q_t above 0.9, make up more than four-fifths of the steps with repeat candidates but carry only about a fifth of the predicted response. Steps with q_t between 0.1 and 0.9 make up about a tenth of those steps but carry about four-fifths of the response, close to their share of the variance (Fig. 3a). This proportionality does not follow from the score alone, which only bounds a step's contribution by the square root of its variance. For these steps, the share of the response divided by the share of the variance had a median of 0.96 (exploratory; Table C1).

[P35] The penalty acts at both ends of a repetitive stretch. About half of the change in ln K came from a lower entry rate and half from a higher exit rate, in the smaller and the larger models alike (Fig. 3b). The derivative predicted this split less well than the total. It put slightly more of the response on the exit rate than was measured, and the difference excluded zero for two of the nine models (Appendix F10).

## V. Discussion

[P37] In the registered tests, the derivative estimated from unpenalized samples predicted the change in switching, and in an exploratory analysis a prediction from the design articles alone carried over to new articles (Appendix F4). The prediction depends on how far each choice departed from the model's own probability of repeating, not on the number of repeats alone. The compensator Q is the discrete-time counterpart of the escape-rate term in response theory for jump processes [0902.3955; 2005.10503], although that theory separates terms by time reversal, which autoregressive sampling lacks.

[P38] The first-order prediction held at penalties with |h*|/T* between 0.7 and 2.1. There, the exact probability ratios of the unpenalized samples concentrated on few samples, with an effective sample size of 1.3 to 2.1 out of four per prompt (Appendix D). We attribute the contrast to scale, without having tested it. For a whole text, the probability ratio is a product over 512 steps, so small changes at many steps compound. ln K is instead the log of a ratio of counts summed over many steps and texts. Each step changes it only slightly, so its response stays close to linear.

[P39] The equilibrium two-state model of Sec. III D depends on the energy assigned to a step, which the sampler does not define. With −log p as the energy it overstated the effect by about 45%; with the logits it understated it, or predicted the wrong sign (Appendix H).

[P40] The derivative of Sec. III A applies to any small additive change to the logits, such as logit biases and watermarking biases [2301.10226]. It also carries over to truncated sampling such as min-p [2407.01082] or top-p [1904.09751] if q_t is taken within the truncated set (Appendix G). We tested only the two repetition penalties, without truncation. Locating T_× required unpenalized runs at several temperatures, comparable in cost to trying a few penalty strengths, but the same runs give the response to any such change.

[P41] Because the tests estimated the derivative from their own unpenalized runs, they test whether the first-order prediction holds at h*. A prediction made before any test data existed was examined only in an exploratory analysis (Appendix F4). The confirmation rests on one replication after an undecided first test and on one test of two larger models, and the additive, multiplicative and larger-model tests share the same 120 articles. Decisions concerned the median over the seven smaller models or the mean over the two larger ones, and the bootstrap intervals resample articles, not models. All prospective tests used English news articles and base models of up to 7.6 billion parameters. Runs on novels, scientific papers and code are in Appendix F9. For instruction-tuned models the test did not decide, and the widely used presence and frequency penalties were not tested. For the multiplicative penalty, the prediction held only up to the weakest divisors used in practice (Appendix F5). The two states are a labeling of a continuum, K averages over a drift along the text, and s_pen depends on the length of the text. The shift of T_× itself, which a user would target, did not always match the intended 0.10, for reasons we have not fully identified (Sec. IV B). Model revisions were not pinned in the code, although the revisions used can be identified (Appendix A). The code and the derivations were written by an AI assistant under the author's direction. They were checked by tests, an independent reimplementation and other AI instances, not line by line by the author (Appendix E).
